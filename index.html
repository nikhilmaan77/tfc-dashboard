Question 1

Our supply chain strategy

The Fresh Connection sells chilled fruit juice under service level agreements. The product is functional rather than innovative — demand is stable and predictable, margins are thin, and the differentiator is reliability rather than variety. That points to an efficient, cost-leadership supply chain.

But perishability and the contract penalty structure pull the other way. A stockout or a short-dated delivery is punished immediately through the contract index, and there is no way to recover it later.

Our declared strategy: cost leadership with a reliability floor. We compete as the low-cost, dependable supplier of a commodity juice, not on responsiveness. We do not chase promotional upside and we do not carry speculative inventory. What we do buy without compromise is enough reliability to hit the service levels we have contracted — because the penalty for missing them exceeds the cost of preventing them by a wide margin.

Where our behaviour actually sat

A strategy is what shows up in the decisions, not what is written on the slide. Judged that way, our first three rounds were three different strategies.

The trajectory

We got worse before we got better

Round 1 was the weakest round of the game and Round 2 only partially repaired it. The turnaround is entirely a Round 3 event — and it came from removing penalties, not from selling more.

The mechanism

The promise gap

Penalties were never a sales problem or a supply problem in isolation. They were a gap problem — the distance between what we contracted and what the chain could actually deliver.

Contracted index Attained — wide gap Attained — gap closed

In Round 2 we contracted a Food & Groceries index of 1.0394 and attained 0.7394. In Round 3 we contracted 1.0079 and attained 0.9790.

We did not sell less in Round 3. We sold what we could actually deliver, and realized revenue rose by €470,656.

How the turnaround happened

A swing of €601,492 in operating profit. Almost all of it is penalty recovery.

Contracted sales revenue barely moved across all four rounds — within a 3.4% band. As a team we have not yet grown the business. Every euro of improvement came from removing penalties and cost, which is worth knowing, because it means the revenue upside is still entirely untouched.

Question 3

KPIs achieved and missed

Note 1. Round 3 service level of 94.4% against a 95% promise is a miss on paper, but the residual penalty of €48,884 is 1.9% of contracted revenue — within the range where the index premium earned exceeds the penalty paid. We treat it as met.

Note 2. The Round 1 obsolescence result is a false positive. It fell because Vitamin C shortages prevented us building C-power stock that would later have expired, not because shelf life was managed well.

Functional scores

The purchasing score moved against us deliberately. The 0.9678 index in Round 1 was the best functional purchasing score and the worst company result of the game. On components with thin supplier free capacity, that functional metric and team ROI pull in opposite directions — the clearest example in three rounds of a functional KPI that has to be overridden by team strategy.
Question 4

Rounds 4 to 6

Continue cost leadership with a reliability floor, and begin monetising the reliability we have built. We have proven the chain can hold 94.4% service at 3.4% obsolescence. Rounds 4 to 6 carry higher weight in the final score, so consistency matters more than experimentation — but we have never once tried to grow revenue, and that is where the remaining upside sits.

  1. Change one thing per function per round.
  2. Never raise a promise before the capability appears in the previous round's results.
  3. Protect the €48,884 penalty level as a floor, not a target.
